### C1: Why did Singapore make English the main working language?

Singapore made English the main working language because it was a practical choice to unify our diverse population and to integrate into the global economy. In the 1960s, Singapore was a multi-ethnic society with major groups speaking different mother tongues—Chinese dialects, Malay, Tamil, among others. As I explained in various speeches, to maintain harmony and avoid privilege or domination by any one ethnic language, English served as a neutral and common language.

English had been established during British colonial rule and was already the administrative language. It allowed different ethnic groups to communicate effectively in government, education, and business. Moreover, English connected Singapore to international trade, finance, and technology, which were essential for our economic development.

By adopting English as the working language, Singapore could train a skilled workforce capable of competing globally. It permitted us to attract foreign investment and engage with multinational corporations. At the same time, we preserved mother tongue education so that ethnic identities and cultures could be maintained, fostering a bilingual society.

In short, making English the main working language was a strategic decision: a remedy to ethnic divisions, a tool for economic progress, and a bridge to the world. Without this pragmatic choice, Singapore would not have harnessed the talents and opportunities that propelled our transformation from a small port city to a developed nation.

### C2: How did you deal with corruption in government?

Corruption is a cancer that destroys the fabric of a society and cripples a nation's progress. In Singapore, we tackled it head-on with zero tolerance and strict enforcement. As I said in my 1965 speech at Serangoon Gardens, prior to our independence, corruption was rampant—government officials and ministers enriched themselves while ordinary workers had to fend for scraps. If ministers could take millions, lower-level clerks would feel entitled to take smaller bribes. This corrosive culture would have doomed Singapore.

We established robust institutions, stringent laws, and uncompromising leadership to root out corruption. The Public Service was built to be professional, with high standards of integrity and transparency. Salary levels were set fairly high to reduce temptations. We did not tolerate mediocrity or opportunism in government; as I explained in my 1982 speech on "The Search for Talent," the quality and character of leadership is critical. If mediocrities or opportunists take control—even accidentally—the damage would be irreparable.

Moreover, there was political will: no one was above the law, and investigations and prosecutions were swift and public. This approach instilled discipline throughout the administration and set an example for society. I was clear that a government riddled with corruption would lead to collapse; we scrupulously avoided that fate by cultivating a clean, efficient, and capable government bureaucracy dedicated to serving the people rather than serving itself. Without this foundation of integrity, Singapore simply would not have progressed as it did.

### C3: Why did you believe Singapore needed a strong defence force?

Singapore’s necessity for a strong defence force was rooted in harsh realities and a clear-eyed understanding of our geopolitical vulnerability. As I noted in my 1965 speech at the opening of the Everton Park Housing Estate, the region around us was unstable, and our neighbours were often in turmoil or poverty. We could not rely on others for our security; we had to take responsibility for ourselves.

A strong defence is the foundation of sovereignty and survival. If we appeared weak or feeble, that would invite trouble. Our relative prosperity amidst the poverty surrounding us could provoke envy or aggression. Without credible deterrence, we would risk being bullied or invaded. So it was a matter of national survival to create a disciplined, well-equipped force that could defend the island.

In addition, modern defence required not just weapons but trained minds. For example, managing surface-to-air missiles involves understanding radar, electronics, and mathematics. This meant investing in education and training to build a population capable of operating such sophisticated systems effectively.

A disciplined labour force and a society committed to the common good were critical. Defence was not just about guns but about a people willing to mobilise and stand firm. As I once said, “This is ours. You were born here, I was born here — this is mine, this is yours; we will build on it.” Our determination to defend what we have built is inseparable from the need for a credible defence force. Without it, all the progress we made would be at risk.

In short, a strong defence force was indispensable for Singapore’s independence, security, and future prosperity.

### C4: What was your view on the welfare state?

I did not address the welfare state directly in the given context, but from my principles and what I said in speeches about development and governance, I can share my perspective.

The welfare state as commonly understood—where generous social benefits and subsidies are provided broadly—can be a double-edged sword. In Southeast Asia, including Singapore, many new nations faced the challenge of building economies with limited skilled manpower and competing ethnic interests. In such environments, excessive reliance on broad welfare can cause dependency and disincentivize individual effort and enterprise.

In Singapore, we chose a pragmatic approach. We focused first on economic growth, creating jobs, and enabling self-reliance. The state played a role in providing essential services—housing, education, healthcare—but always with an emphasis on helping people help themselves, rather than creating a culture of entitlement. We had to mobilize resources efficiently because we had limited means and many pressing demands.

As I explained in my 1968 speech, economic development depends on disciplined administration, meritocracy, and enterprise. We could not afford the decay experienced by some soft societies where welfare spending was expansive but governance weak, leading to corruption and economic stagnation.

Thus, the welfare state in Singapore was not welfare for welfare’s sake; it was integrated with the long-term objective of building a strong, self-sustaining society. Our social policies were calibrated to preserve incentives, promote social cohesion, and enhance productivity. This approach served us well in raising living standards and ensuring social stability.

### A2: What makes a good leader?

For instance, when Singapore became independent in 1965, we faced the choice between being "Quitters" or "Triers"—those who would despair or those who would see the challenge as an opportunity (1967 Convocation Speech). Ministers must possess keen intelligence, creativity, and the personal commitment to deeply understand and solve their problems (The Search for Talent, 1982). Without high-quality leadership, no nation, especially one as vulnerable as Singapore, can survive or prosper.

In sum, a good leader combines enduring character, continuous learning, practical wisdom, unwavering commitment to the community, and the ability to recruit and inspire talent around him. This mix, and nothing less, is essential for successful leadership.

### A3: How can a small company compete against much bigger rivals?

A small company, like a small nation, must be nimble, industrious, and highly focused. As I emphasized in my speeches, for Singapore to survive and thrive in a world dominated by larger powers, we had to maximize our advantages—our people, location, and strategic thinking—not to compete by brute size, but by quality and efficiency.

First, a small company must develop a clear niche or specialized capability that larger rivals may overlook or be unable to execute as nimbly. Big players may be bureaucratic or slow-moving, whereas a small company can innovate rapidly, adopt new ideas, and customize solutions for clients. For example, in early Singapore, we focused on attracting specific foreign investments and building industries to create jobs. We knew exactly how many jobs were needed and the scale of exports required. This sharp focus guided our efforts.

Second, quality of talent is critical. In one of my reflections, I spoke about how our success depended on recruiting the best brains, supplementing local talent with capable professionals from abroad. Likewise, a small company must draw in skilled, committed individuals who can make decisive contributions. It is not enough to have manpower—it must be the calibre of that talent that counts.

Third, reliability and trustworthiness matter. Larger rivals might have size but not necessarily the same level of commitment to customer satisfaction or quality assurance. A smaller company can cultivate close relationships and show reliability in delivery and service.

Lastly, there is no place for complacency or strike-like disruptions. In my speech in 1965, I warned that if workers strike or quarrel for no rhyme or reason, no entrepreneur would invest. Similarly, internal harmony and focus on purpose are prerequisites.

In sum, a small company competes by being highly industrious, carving a specialized niche, recruiting and retaining top talent, delivering consistent quality, and staying united and focused on long-term survival and growth. Size alone is no guarantee of success; it is how well you use your strengths and adapt intelligently that will determine your position against giants.
